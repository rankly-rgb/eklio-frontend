# How somatic experiencing therapy works

*By Priya Natarajan, LMFT 120455*

Understanding how somatic experiencing therapy works can make a body-focused approach feel less abstract before you decide whether to discuss it with a therapist. Somatic experiencing is a form of therapy that pays attention to present-moment physical sensations alongside thoughts, emotions, memories, and daily circumstances. Rather than requiring a complete retelling of difficult events, it may involve noticing manageable pieces of experience and moving back and forth between what feels activating and what feels more neutral or steady. The conversation remains important; the body is another source of information within it.

## What does somatic experiencing focus on during a session?

Somatic experiencing focuses on how experiences may show up in the body in the present, not only on the story of what happened. A therapist might invite attention to sensations such as tightness in the chest, a clenched jaw, warmth, shakiness, numbness, a sinking feeling, or an urge to move. There is no single correct sensation to notice, and a person does not need to have clear language for body cues at the outset.

The purpose of noticing is not to force a sensation to change or to assign it a dramatic meaning. It is to describe what is there with care and specificity. A therapist may ask where a sensation is located, whether it has an edge or direction, whether it shifts when it is noticed, or what else is present at the same time. Thoughts, images, emotions, and memories can all be part of the discussion.

This approach distinguishes between the content of an experience and the immediate experience of it. For example, someone might talk about a tense meeting at work while also noticing held breath, pressure behind the eyes, or feet pressing into the floor. Both the event and the current body response can be relevant. The therapist helps keep attention oriented to what is happening now rather than assuming that one explanation fits every reaction.

## Why is the work done in small pieces?

Somatic experiencing generally approaches difficult material in small, manageable amounts rather than asking someone to stay with the most intense part of an experience for a long stretch. This pacing is sometimes called titration, meaning the therapist and client work with a small amount at a time. It can include briefly noticing an upsetting memory, image, feeling, or sensation and then shifting attention to something less charged.

The shift is not a distraction from the subject. It is part of observing how the nervous system responds when attention moves between different experiences. A therapist may invite someone to notice a supportive chair, the temperature of the room, a neutral object, a part of the body that feels less activated, or an aspect of the present day that is distinct from the past. What counts as neutral or steady is individual and may change from moment to moment.

Small pieces also leave room for choice. A person can say when a question feels too close, when a sensation is difficult to describe, or when they would prefer to remain with conversation rather than body attention. Therapy does not require pushing through because an experience seems important. The pace and focus are topics to discuss openly, including when the work concerns something that has been carried quietly for a long time.

## How are activation and settling noticed?

Activation and settling are noticed by tracking changes in attention, body sensations, emotion, and energy as they occur. Activation can refer to signs that something feels more charged, such as faster speech, restlessness, muscle tension, a racing heart, blankness, or difficulty staying oriented to the conversation. Settling can refer to signs of more ease, steadiness, or contact with the present, though it does not have to look the same for everyone.

A therapist may periodically ask what is happening right now rather than assuming that a calm appearance means little is going on. Someone may appear composed while noticing a great deal internally, particularly when they are used to functioning through pressure. Likewise, a visible emotional response is not the only sign that an experience matters. The work makes room for both subtle and obvious shifts.

One part of this process is orienting, which means noticing features of the current environment. Looking around the room, noticing light or sound, feeling contact with a chair, or naming the date can help establish a present-time reference point. These observations are not tests and do not need to be performed perfectly. They offer information about where attention is resting at a given moment.

A therapist may track several kinds of information at once:

- Physical sensations, movement impulses, posture, and breathing
- Emotions, images, words, and memories that arise alongside a sensation
- Signs of attention narrowing, drifting, or returning to the room
- What feels neutral, familiar, supportive, or simply less charged in the moment

## What might a somatic experiencing appointment include?

A somatic experiencing appointment may include ordinary conversation, discussion of current concerns, and brief invitations to notice present-moment body cues. The session does not need to begin with a particular memory or a physical exercise. A therapist may start by asking what has been most present in daily life, what brought someone in, or what they hope to understand more clearly through therapy.

As a topic develops, the therapist might slow down at certain moments. They may ask what happens in the body while a person describes a situation, whether there is an impulse to move or pull away, or what changes when attention returns to the room. At other times, the session may remain largely conversational. Body awareness is an option for exploring experience, not a requirement to set aside words, analysis, or context.

The session can also include practical discussion of professional demands, relationships, sleep, routines, and current stressors. A body-focused approach does not treat these circumstances as separate from emotional life. It considers how the pace and pressures of daily life may be reflected in attention, posture, energy, and other immediate cues. At Natarajan Therapy, Priya Natarajan, LMFT, describes her work as EMDR with room for the body, particularly for professionals who have outgrown only talking about what happened.

## Situations people may bring to somatic experiencing

People may bring a range of current and past experiences to somatic experiencing, including events that still register physically or emotionally in day-to-day life. The focus might be a specific incident, a repeating interpersonal pattern, persistent pressure at work, a difficult transition, or a reaction that is easier to notice in the body than explain in words. The approach does not require a person to choose one category before beginning a conversation.

Some people are interested in this style of therapy because talking has helped them understand a situation but has not fully addressed the way it continues to show up moment to moment. Others may prefer to start with talking and add body awareness only when it seems relevant. Either preference can be discussed with a therapist without committing to a particular method.

A consultation is a useful time to ask how somatic experiencing would be used in the work, what kinds of prompts the therapist tends to offer, and how preferences are handled. It can also be useful to name prior experiences with therapy, including what felt useful, what felt overly intense, and what you do not want assumed. Clear information matters when considering any therapeutic approach.

## Questions to bring to a consultation

Questions about pacing, body attention, and how a therapist makes decisions are appropriate to bring to a consultation. Somatic experiencing can sound unfamiliar, especially if prior therapy has centered primarily on insight, problem-solving, or conversation. Asking for plain descriptions can clarify what the approach would and would not ask of you.

You can ask how the therapist introduces attention to sensations, what happens if you do not notice much in your body, and how they respond when discussing a subject becomes too activating. It may also be helpful to ask how they distinguish between time for open conversation and time for more focused observation. The answers should give you a clearer picture of the therapist's style and of the choices available during sessions.

If EMDR is also part of the conversation, ask how the therapist understands the relationship between EMDR and body-aware work in their practice. Priya Natarajan's stated focus is steady EMDR for adults who appear fine on the outside while an old experience remains present every day. A consultation can be a place to discuss whether that framing, somatic experiencing, or another conversational focus fits what you want to bring forward.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to know the source of a body reaction before discussing it?

No, you do not need to identify the source before discussing a body reaction. A therapist can begin with what you notice in the present and stay curious about the context without forcing a conclusion. Memories or explanations may emerge in conversation, but they are not a prerequisite.

### What does titration mean in body-focused therapy?

Titration means working with a small amount of difficult material at a time. It may involve briefly noticing an activated sensation, memory, or emotion and then returning attention to something more neutral or present-day. The therapist uses this pacing to keep track of what is occurring during the conversation.

### Can I speak up if a therapist's prompt does not work for me?

Yes, you can say that a prompt feels unclear, uncomfortable, too fast, or simply unhelpful. You can also ask to stay with talking, change the subject, or take time to orient to the room. These responses provide useful information for the therapeutic conversation.

### Is body awareness the same as analyzing every physical symptom?

No, body awareness in therapy is not the same as analyzing or explaining every physical symptom. It involves noticing immediate sensations and their context as one part of the session. Medical questions and concerns about physical symptoms are separate matters to discuss with an appropriate medical professional.

### What should I listen for when a therapist explains their approach?

Listen for a clear description of what the therapist might invite you to notice, how they handle pacing, and how your preferences guide the work. It is reasonable to ask for examples of the kinds of questions they use. You can also ask how they incorporate conversation, current circumstances, and body cues without making assumptions about your experience.
