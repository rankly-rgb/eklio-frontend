# How EMDR Therapy Works

*By Priya Natarajan, LMFT 120455*

Understanding how EMDR therapy works can make a structured, sometimes unfamiliar therapy approach easier to consider. EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. It is a psychotherapy approach that uses focused attention, memory work, and alternating forms of stimulation while a therapist guides the process. At Natarajan Therapy, Priya Natarajan, LMFT, offers EMDR with room for the body, particularly for adults who look fine from the outside and still encounter the old thing in daily life.

## The eight phases of EMDR therapy

EMDR therapy is organized into eight phases that give the work a clear structure rather than moving directly into difficult material. The phases provide a way to discuss relevant history, identify present-day concerns, prepare for processing, work with selected material, and review what is happening over time. They are not a rigid script, and they do not all take the same amount of attention in every course of therapy.

The first phases involve conversation and planning. A therapist asks about current life, earlier experiences that may be relevant, patterns that arise now, and practical factors that affect how therapy is approached. Preparation can include discussing ways to orient to the present, pause, communicate limits, and notice when attention is becoming too narrow or too flooded.

Later phases involve processing a selected target and checking in on thoughts, emotions, images, and physical sensations. A target may include a specific memory, an image, a belief about oneself connected with an experience, or a present trigger linked to older material. The final phases include reevaluation, meaning the therapist and client return to previously addressed material to notice what is present and decide what, if anything, needs further attention.

The eight phases are commonly described as:

- History taking and treatment planning
- Preparation and orientation to the process
- Assessment of a selected target
- Desensitization through processing
- Installation of a preferred belief or perspective
- Body scan
- Closure at the end of a session
- Reevaluation at a later session

## What is dual attention in EMDR?

Dual attention means keeping one part of awareness in the present while another part attends to selected material from the past. During EMDR, a person is not asked to become fully absorbed in a memory as though it were happening again. Instead, the therapist invites attention to an image, thought, emotion, or body sensation while also maintaining contact with the room, the therapist, and the present moment.

This present-day anchor matters because EMDR is not simply retelling an event in detail. The therapist may periodically ask what the person notices now, rather than asking for a complete account of what happened. What arises may be a shift in image, a thought, an emotion, a physical sensation, an unrelated memory, or no immediately clear change. Each response is information that can be noticed without needing to force a particular direction.

The pace is collaborative. A person can say when they need to slow down, stop, ask a question, or shift attention back to the room. Priya’s approach makes room for body-based information alongside words, particularly when someone has spent a long time understanding an experience intellectually but finds that the experience still appears in reactions, tension, or familiar professional patterns.

## Why does EMDR use alternating stimulation?

EMDR uses alternating stimulation to accompany focused attention during processing. This is often called bilateral stimulation because the stimulation alternates from one side of the body or field of attention to the other. It can take different forms, including following a therapist’s hand movements with the eyes, alternating taps, or alternating tones.

A therapist and client can discuss which form is practical and comfortable before using it. Eye movements are one option, not a requirement. The stimulation is offered in brief sets, and the therapist pauses between sets to ask what is being noticed. This stop-and-check rhythm distinguishes the process from an uninterrupted exercise.

No one is expected to produce the right response during a set. EMDR does not depend on giving a polished explanation or arriving at a particular insight. The therapist tracks the person’s responses, asks brief questions when useful, and helps keep the selected focus connected to the present context. If the material becomes unclear, the work may return to orientation, conversation, or a different aspect of the target.

## How thoughts, emotions, and body cues are used in EMDR

EMDR attends to thoughts, emotions, images, and body cues because an experience can show up through more than one channel at once. Before processing a target, a therapist may ask what image represents it, what belief about oneself feels connected to it, what emotion is present, and where the person notices anything physically. These questions are invitations to observe, not tests of memory or self-awareness.

A belief in EMDR is usually a short statement that carries personal meaning, such as an old conclusion about responsibility, danger, control, or worth. The therapist may also ask about a perspective the person would rather hold in relation to the experience. This does not mean replacing a difficult thought with forced positivity. It provides language for identifying the meaning attached to a target and for noticing whether that meaning shifts during the work.

Body awareness can be simple and concrete. It might involve noticing tightness in the jaw, a heavy feeling in the chest, heat in the face, a restless urge to move, or an absence of clear sensation. At Natarajan Therapy, body cues can be included without making them the only focus. For professionals who have outgrown just talking about it, this can create room to notice information that does not arrive first as a narrative.

## What does a processing set look like?

A processing set is a short interval of bilateral stimulation followed by a pause to notice what is present. The therapist begins by helping the person identify a workable focus, then invites them to hold that focus in mind while using the agreed form of alternating stimulation. After the set, the therapist commonly asks an open question such as what the person noticed.

The response may be brief or detailed. A person might report an image, emotion, bodily sensation, thought, memory connection, or uncertainty. The therapist generally follows what arises rather than deciding in advance what the material should mean. At times, a therapist may offer a prompt that brings attention back to the target, the present moment, or a particular belief that was identified earlier.

A session also includes closure. Closure is not a requirement to reach a certain endpoint before leaving; it is a part of the structure that helps mark where the session is ending and what may be useful to note afterward. The therapist may discuss what to observe between appointments, including dreams, emotions, body sensations, or memories that come to mind. These observations can inform later conversations without requiring a person to analyze every experience alone.

## How EMDR is used when experiences are layered or ongoing

EMDR can be organized around a series of related experiences when there is not one single event that explains what is happening now. A therapist may work with a current situation that carries a familiar charge, then explore whether it connects with earlier memories, recurring themes, or learned conclusions about oneself and other people. The focus remains specific enough to work with, even when the broader context is complex.

Ongoing stressors can also be part of the discussion. EMDR does not require treating every present difficulty as a memory from the past. Work may include looking at current situations, the reactions they bring up, and the practical context around them. A therapist considers whether processing is appropriate at a given point and whether more preparation, stabilization, or ordinary talk therapy is the better fit for the moment.

It is reasonable to ask how a therapist decides what to address, how they respond when several topics compete for attention, and how they include the body without pressuring someone to focus on sensations. Those questions clarify the therapist’s approach to structure and collaboration. They can also help distinguish EMDR from a generic expectation that therapy must involve either only talking or only revisiting the past.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is EMDR the same as hypnosis?

No, EMDR is not hypnosis. During EMDR, the person remains aware of the setting and can speak, ask questions, pause, or decline a direction in the work. The therapist uses focused attention and alternating stimulation, not a hypnotic induction.

### Why might an EMDR therapist ask for a number rating?

A number rating is a brief way to track how distressing a selected target seems in a particular moment. It gives the therapist one piece of information alongside words, emotions, images, and body cues. A rating is not a score to achieve or a measure of doing therapy correctly.

### What if nothing noticeable comes up during a processing set?

Nothing immediately noticeable is a valid response to report. The therapist may pause, clarify the focus, ask about subtle observations, or shift back to discussion and grounding. EMDR does not require a dramatic memory or a vivid image in every set.

### Can a person pause EMDR in the middle of a session?

Yes, a person can ask to pause or stop at any point in an EMDR session. The therapist can redirect attention to the present, discuss what is happening, and decide collaboratively what comes next. Consent and communication remain part of the process throughout.

### Does EMDR require believing every thought that comes up?

No, EMDR does not require treating every thought or image as fact. Thoughts that arise are noticed as part of the material connected to a target. The therapist can help distinguish observation, memory, interpretation, and present-day context without arguing someone into a particular conclusion.
