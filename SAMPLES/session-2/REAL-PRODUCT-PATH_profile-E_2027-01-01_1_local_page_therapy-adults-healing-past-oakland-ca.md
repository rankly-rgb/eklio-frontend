## How EMDR and IFS can be used

EMDR and IFS are two therapy approaches that can be used to explore the relationship between past experiences and present-day responses. They offer different ways of organizing the conversation, and either approach may be discussed in relation to what you want to bring into therapy.

EMDR, or Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, is a structured approach that may involve bringing attention to a selected memory, image, belief, emotion, or body sensation while using a form of alternating attention. The details of what is discussed and how attention is guided are part of the collaborative planning process.

IFS, or Internal Family Systems, uses the idea of parts as a practical way to talk about different inner responses. For example, a person may notice one response that keeps moving through responsibilities, another that wants distance from a memory, and another that is concerned about what will happen if things slow down. Parts are used as language for observation, not as a requirement to think of yourself as separate people.

These approaches can be discussed separately or alongside one another. The conversation can remain grounded in ordinary language, with room to ask what a term means or to describe an experience without using therapy vocabulary.

## Working at a patient pace

A patient pace means that therapy is organized with attention to what can be considered in the session and what is pressing in daily life. For people whose schedules and responsibilities do not pause, pacing is not an afterthought; it is part of how the work is discussed.

This may involve spending time understanding the current situation before focusing closely on a particular memory. It may also involve returning to present-day concerns, noticing what feels manageable to discuss, and being clear about whether a topic needs more context. There is no requirement to provide a complete life history in one conversation.

Jordan Ellis uses a measured approach to EMDR and IFS for adults who have been carrying a great deal for others. You can name competing demands, uncertainty about where to begin, and concerns about opening a topic while life remains busy.
