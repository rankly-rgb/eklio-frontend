# What to expect in a first CBT therapy session

*By Dr. Ines Alvarez, PSY 28456*

Knowing what to expect in a first CBT therapy session can make it easier to arrive with a general sense of the purpose, even if you are unsure where to begin. An initial appointment is usually a conversation about what has brought you to therapy, the situations and patterns you want to understand, and whether a structured, skills-based approach makes sense for your goals. You do not need polished answers or a complete history prepared in advance. The therapist’s role is to ask focused questions, explain the process, and begin developing a shared picture of what you would like to address.

## What is covered at a first CBT therapy appointment?

A first CBT appointment usually covers your current concerns, relevant background, goals, and the practical framework for working together. The conversation may start with what led you to schedule therapy now, what has been difficult lately, and how those difficulties affect work, relationships, routines, sleep, concentration, or other parts of daily life.

A therapist may ask about the situations where a concern shows up most clearly. For example, you might discuss an upcoming presentation that prompts intense self-criticism, a conflict you keep replaying, avoidance of a task, or a pattern of overcommitting. The goal is not to produce a perfect account. It is to identify useful starting points and understand the context around them.

There is also typically time for administrative and informed-consent information. This may include privacy and its limits, office policies, records, communication boundaries, payment arrangements, and what to do in an urgent mental health situation. If you have questions about any of this, it is appropriate to ask for clarification.

The first meeting is also an opportunity to describe relevant history at a manageable pace. A therapist may ask about previous therapy, medical or mental health care, medications, family background, major life events, substance use, and support systems when those topics are relevant. You can say if a question feels unclear or if you would rather return to a topic later.

## How much personal history do I need to share?

You only need to share enough personal history to begin understanding the concern and its context. Early questions about your background are meant to help the therapist see connections among present situations, learned expectations, important relationships, and prior coping strategies.

CBT often pays close attention to current patterns, but current patterns do not exist in isolation. A therapist may ask about experiences that shaped how you respond to criticism, uncertainty, conflict, mistakes, or pressure. That does not mean every first session needs to become a detailed review of your past.

You can be direct about your pace and boundaries. For instance, you might say that there is a topic you want the therapist to know exists but do not want to discuss in detail yet. You can also correct an assumption, ask why a question is relevant, or let the therapist know that you need a moment to think. Good information gathering is collaborative, not an interrogation.

## How does a CBT therapist make sense of the problem?

A CBT therapist begins by looking for the links among situations, thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, and actions. This is sometimes called a formulation: a working map of a pattern that may be maintaining distress or getting in the way of what matters to you.

For example, a situation might be receiving brief feedback from a manager. The immediate interpretation could be that you have disappointed someone, followed by anxiety or shame, a tense body, and an urge to reread messages or work late to prevent further criticism. Another person could encounter the same feedback and interpret it differently. CBT is interested in the particular sequence that occurs for you, rather than assuming one meaning fits everyone.

The therapist may ask for a recent, specific example rather than only a broad description such as “I am stressed.” Specific examples make it easier to notice the sequence: what happened, what went through your mind, what emotion appeared, what you did next, and what happened afterward. This information helps guide the focus of future sessions.

A first appointment may include an initial formulation, but it is not a final verdict about you. It can be revised as more context emerges and as you notice details between sessions.

## Will the first session be structured?

The first session is usually structured, while still allowing room for the concerns that matter most to you. CBT commonly uses a clear agenda so that therapy time has a shared focus rather than relying only on wherever the conversation happens to go.

At the beginning, the therapist may check in about what you most want to cover and explain what needs to be included in an initial appointment. During the conversation, they may pause to summarize what they are hearing, ask whether their understanding is accurate, or point out a pattern worth examining. Near the end, there may be a brief recap of the main themes and a discussion of possible next areas of focus.

Structure does not mean rigidly following a script or forcing every issue into a worksheet. It means making the purpose of the conversation visible. If something important comes up unexpectedly, the agenda can be adjusted. If you are new to therapy, the therapist can explain why they are asking focused questions or returning to a particular example.

At Alvarez Psychology, Dr. Ines Alvarez describes her work as practical, skills-based CBT for adults who want to understand their patterns and leave each session with something to try. Her approach is structured CBT for professionals and first-time clients who want a clear plan more than open-ended talk.

## What should I bring up or ask during the first meeting?

Bring up the concerns, preferences, and practical questions that would help you decide whether the working style makes sense for you. You do not need a formal list, but notes can be useful if you tend to forget important details when speaking with someone new.

It can help to mention what you hope therapy will make room for, including concerns that may seem unrelated at first. You might want to discuss work pressure alongside relationship tension, a recurring pattern of avoidance, an upcoming transition, or a reaction that seems out of proportion to the situation. Sharing what feels most pressing gives the therapist a starting point.

You can also ask how the therapist approaches goal setting, how they decide what to focus on, how progress is reviewed, and what participation between appointments may look like. Questions about privacy, records, communication, cancellations, and professional boundaries are equally appropriate. The first meeting is partly an information-gathering conversation for both people.

Useful topics to raise include:

- What prompted you to seek therapy at this point
- A recent situation that captures the pattern you want to understand
- Any prior experiences with counseling or therapy that felt useful or unhelpful
- Preferences about structure, pace, directness, or feedback
- Questions about how goals and session priorities are decided

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to prepare anything before an initial CBT appointment?

No formal preparation is required. It may be useful to jot down a few recent examples, concerns, medications, previous therapy experiences, or questions you do not want to forget. You can also arrive without notes and begin with what feels most relevant that day.

### What if I become emotional during the session?

Emotional reactions can be part of discussing difficult experiences or patterns. You can tell the therapist what is happening, pause, ask for a moment, or shift the conversation if needed. The therapist can help keep track of the discussion and pace it appropriately.

### Can I say that I am not sure therapy is right for me?

Yes. Uncertainty about therapy, a particular approach, or the fit with a therapist is appropriate to discuss openly. Naming that uncertainty can lead to a clearer conversation about your expectations, concerns, and what the therapist’s approach involves.

### What if I have more than one issue I want to discuss?

You can mention all of the concerns that feel important. The first meeting may identify how they connect and which area makes the most sense to begin with. Choosing an initial focus does not mean other concerns are being dismissed.

### Can I bring notes to my first appointment?

Yes, notes are welcome if they help you organize your thoughts. You might write down situations, questions, prior care, medications, or goals you want to mention. The notes do not need to be detailed or complete.
