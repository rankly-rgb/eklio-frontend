# IFS therapy for adults healing from the past

*By Jordan Ellis, LMFT 121877*

IFS therapy for adults healing from the past is a way of talking about the different reactions, emotions, and inner roles that may show up when old experiences still matter in the present. At Ellis Counseling, Jordan Ellis, LMFT, uses IFS with adults, including people who spend much of their time caring for others and have had little room to put down what they carry. This article explains the basic ideas behind IFS, how the conversations are structured, and what may be useful to consider when exploring this approach.

## How does IFS use the idea of parts?

IFS uses “parts” as everyday language for the different sides of a person that may react, protect, worry, plan, criticize, withdraw, or push forward. A part is not a separate personality, and IFS does not require anyone to think of themselves as divided into separate people. It is a framework for noticing that a person can have more than one reaction at once.

For example, one side may want to talk about a difficult experience, while another may want to change the subject, stay busy, or keep emotions tightly contained. Rather than treating that tension as a problem to overcome, IFS gives each response room to be noticed. The conversation can explore what that response is trying to do and what it may be concerned about.

IFS commonly describes parts in broad groups. Protective parts may work proactively through planning, perfectionism, caretaking, pleasing others, or staying highly alert. Other protective responses may appear when something feels too intense, such as shutting down, distracting, becoming angry, or turning toward numbing routines. The model also makes room for younger or more vulnerable feelings that may be connected to painful experiences.

The language is flexible. Someone may describe a part as an inner voice, a physical sensation, a familiar reaction, an image, or simply a pattern that appears in certain situations. Jordan can use the words that make the most sense to the person in the room rather than requiring a particular vocabulary.

## How is IFS different from simply analyzing your thoughts?

IFS is different from thought analysis because it focuses on relating to reactions with curiosity, not only evaluating whether thoughts are accurate. A conversation may include thoughts, but it can also attend to emotions, body sensations, impulses, memories, and the purpose a reaction seems to serve.

In a more analytical discussion, a person might identify a recurring belief and examine the evidence for and against it. IFS may instead ask what part carries that belief, when it tends to show up, what it fears could happen if it stopped, and how it responds when it is noticed. These questions are not meant to force a particular answer. They help organize attention around the experience that is present.

IFS also uses the term Self to describe a steady, observant stance from which a person can notice internal reactions. Self is not presented as a perfect or permanently available state. It is a practical way to describe moments when there is enough room to observe a reaction without being entirely swept into it. If that kind of distance is not available on a given day, the therapist can work with what is present rather than insisting on a certain state of mind.

This approach can be particularly relevant when a reaction seems contradictory. Someone may care deeply about a family member and also feel resentful, exhausted, fearful, or distant. IFS does not ask a person to choose one reaction as the correct one. It treats the competing responses as information that can be approached gradually.

## What does a therapist do during IFS conversations?

An IFS therapist guides attention toward present-moment reactions and helps keep the conversation organized, consensual, and manageable. The therapist may ask about the words a reaction uses, where it is felt in the body, what tends to bring it forward, or what it wants others to understand.

The therapist does not assign parts or tell someone what their internal experience means. Jordan may offer an observation or a question, then check whether it fits. If it does not fit, that information matters too. The person remains the source of knowledge about their own experience.

At times, the conversation may stay with a current situation rather than move toward the past. A demanding week, conflict in a relationship, a caregiving responsibility, or difficulty resting can offer useful information about the roles a person has learned to take on. Looking at a current reaction may also show how an older experience is being echoed without requiring a detailed retelling.

The pace can change throughout the conversation. If a topic begins to feel too intense, the therapist may invite attention back to the room, a neutral subject, a body sensation, or the part that wants distance. This is not a detour from the work. In IFS, hesitation, uncertainty, and protectiveness are all relevant parts of the conversation.

## Can IFS focus on patterns that developed while caring for others?

IFS can focus on caregiving patterns when those roles shape how a person responds to stress, relationships, needs, or memories from the past. Caring for others can involve practical tasks, emotional vigilance, decision-making, and a sense of responsibility that leaves little attention for one’s own internal experience.

A person may notice a part that automatically takes charge, anticipates everyone else’s needs, avoids asking for support, or becomes uncomfortable when someone else is concerned about them. IFS does not assume these reactions are wrong. It looks at how they developed, what they are trying to prevent, and what conditions make them more active.

Current responsibilities do not need to be set aside for the past to be discussed. At Ellis Counseling, the conversation can include the demands that are happening now alongside older experiences. This may be especially important for adults whose schedules and relationships do not pause simply because difficult material has come up in therapy.

Some themes that may be explored through an IFS lens include:

- Feeling responsible for keeping situations stable
- Difficulty recognizing personal limits until stress is already high
- A strong inner rule about being useful, capable, or calm
- Mixed feelings about family roles and obligations
- Reactions that appear when another person needs care or reassurance
- Memories or emotions connected to earlier periods of responsibility

## What should I consider when looking for an IFS therapist?

When looking for an IFS therapist, consider whether the therapist can explain the approach plainly and discuss how it may be used with the concerns you want to bring. It can be useful to ask how the therapist understands parts language, how they decide what to focus on, and how they respond if a topic becomes overwhelming or unclear.

You may also ask how the therapist incorporates present-day pressures into the work. For adults carrying family, work, or caregiving responsibilities, it can matter that therapy has room for both immediate concerns and the older experiences that may be connected to them. A therapist’s answer can give a sense of how structured or flexible their style is.

It is reasonable to ask what a typical conversation looks like, whether the therapist uses exercises or imagery, and whether there is any expectation that you share particular details. You can also ask how the therapist handles uncertainty, because people do not always arrive with a neat account of what happened or a clear label for what they need.

Questions about approach and fit can be part of an initial conversation without requiring a commitment to a particular kind of therapy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why might a part seem critical or controlling?

A critical or controlling part may be trying to prevent mistakes, rejection, conflict, or vulnerability. IFS approaches that response with curiosity about its role rather than assuming it needs to be argued with. The conversation can explore what it is trying to manage and what it expects would happen if it stepped back.

### Can IFS be useful when several memories seem connected?

IFS can provide a way to notice themes that link several memories without requiring them to be sorted into a single story immediately. A therapist may follow the reaction that is most present and see what associations emerge. The focus can remain on the current experience if the details of the memories are uncertain.

### Do I need to use images or names for my parts?

No, images and names are optional. Some people describe a part through a feeling, a thought pattern, a posture, or a sensation in the body. IFS can use whichever description is most natural and does not require creative visualization.

### Can IFS include present-day decisions and relationships?

Yes, IFS can include current decisions, relationships, and responsibilities. Present situations may bring out familiar protective reactions or competing needs. Discussing those situations can be part of understanding how older patterns show up now.

### What happens if the conversation feels stuck?

If the conversation feels stuck, the therapist can slow down and become curious about the part that does not want to continue. Feeling unsure, blank, impatient, or guarded can all be explored as information. The work does not depend on forcing insight or recalling more than feels manageable.
