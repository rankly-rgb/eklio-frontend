# How EMDR Therapy Works: A Plain-Language Guide

*By Jordan Ellis, LMFT 121877*

Understanding how EMDR therapy works can make the approach feel less mysterious before you decide whether it is worth discussing with a therapist. EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, a structured psychotherapy approach that uses attention to memories, present reactions, and bilateral stimulation, such as side-to-side eye movements or alternating taps. At Ellis Counseling, Jordan Ellis, LMFT, offers patient EMDR and IFS for adults whose days are often organized around caring for other people while older experiences continue to take up space.

## The basic structure of EMDR therapy

EMDR therapy is a phased approach that combines conversation, focused attention, and repeated short sets of bilateral stimulation. Rather than asking someone to give a detailed chronological account of every difficult experience, the therapist and client identify a manageable focus and return to it in brief intervals. What is discussed, how much is discussed, and when to pause remain part of the conversation throughout.

The approach is commonly described in eight phases, though therapy does not move through them like a rigid checklist. Early phases involve learning about the concerns bringing someone in, identifying relevant past and current experiences, and discussing ways to stay oriented during difficult material. Later phases may involve reprocessing selected memories and reviewing what comes up between meetings.

The word reprocessing can sound technical. In EMDR, it refers to attending to a memory and the thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, and meanings connected to it while using bilateral stimulation. The therapist checks in repeatedly rather than asking a person to stay with intense material without interruption.

The eight phases are commonly organized around these tasks:

- Learning relevant history and identifying possible areas of focus
- Preparing for the work and discussing grounding or settling practices
- Identifying a specific memory, related belief, emotions, and body sensations
- Using bilateral stimulation while noticing what arises
- Checking whether a different or more useful belief fits the memory
- Noticing any remaining physical sensations connected to the memory
- Closing the session in an oriented, deliberate way
- Reviewing changes, questions, or new material at a later session

## What bilateral stimulation does during EMDR

Bilateral stimulation is an alternating left-right activity used while a person briefly focuses on selected material. Eye movements are one option, but they are not the only form. A therapist may use alternating taps, handheld pulsers, or sounds that move from one side to the other, depending on what is available and what a person prefers.

During a set, the therapist may ask someone simply to notice whatever comes to mind. That could include an image, a fragment of a memory, a thought, an emotion, a body sensation, or an association that initially seems unrelated. After each short set, the therapist asks what was noticed and uses that information to guide the next step.

Bilateral stimulation is not hypnosis, and it does not require a person to surrender awareness or control. A person can speak, ask for clarification, change the type or pace of stimulation, keep their eyes open, or stop a set. The work is collaborative rather than something done to someone.

## Choosing a focus for EMDR work

A focus for EMDR work is selected collaboratively from experiences, present triggers, or recurring themes that matter to the person. The focus may be a distinct event, a period of life, an interaction, an image, or a present situation that brings up an older sense of threat, responsibility, shame, or helplessness. It does not have to be the biggest or most dramatic event a person can name.

The therapist may ask about the image that represents the experience, the negative belief that feels connected to it, and the belief the person would prefer to hold in its place. For example, someone might notice a thought such as “I should have done more” in connection with a past caregiving experience. This is not a test of whether a thought is rational; it helps define what the memory means in the present.

For people who spend much of their time responding to others’ needs, the focus may include the strain of ongoing responsibilities as well as older experiences. Jordan Ellis approaches this work with the understanding that life may not pause simply because therapy is underway. A planned focus can be adjusted when current demands need more attention.

## What happens while a memory is being reprocessed

While a memory is being reprocessed, the therapist guides brief periods of attention to the chosen material and pauses regularly to ask what the person notices. The person may hold an image in mind along with a thought, emotion, or body sensation, then engage in bilateral stimulation for a short set. There is no requirement to force a memory, produce a particular insight, or describe each association out loud.

The therapist follows the person’s responses and helps maintain a workable level of engagement. Sometimes the material remains close to the original memory; sometimes it moves to a related experience, present concern, or bodily response. If the work becomes too much, the therapist can slow down, shift attention to the present, use a grounding exercise, or postpone further reprocessing.

EMDR sessions also include deliberate closure. Closure may involve orienting to the room, noticing the body, reviewing what came up, and identifying how to handle material that may continue to surface afterward. The purpose is not to complete every thread in one meeting. Patient pacing matters, particularly when someone is balancing therapy with demanding family or caregiving roles.

## How EMDR and IFS can be part of the same therapy

EMDR and IFS can be used together when a therapist and client decide that both approaches fit the work being discussed. IFS, or Internal Family Systems, is an approach that uses the idea of inner parts, such as a protective, critical, worried, or overfunctioning side of a person. In an EMDR context, IFS language can sometimes help identify concerns, understand hesitation, or create more clarity about what needs attention before reprocessing.

Using both approaches does not mean every session follows the same format. One meeting might focus primarily on current demands, decision-making, or an inner conflict. Another might include EMDR preparation or bilateral stimulation. The therapist can also stay with ordinary language rather than using formal terms if that is more natural for the person.

When considering a therapist, it can be useful to ask how they decide between conversation, preparation, EMDR reprocessing, and other approaches. You can also ask how they handle pauses, strong reactions, changing responsibilities, and uncertainty about where to begin. Clear answers can help you understand the structure of the work without requiring you to have a complete plan beforehand.

Questions that may be useful to bring include:

- How do you decide what to focus on in EMDR?
- What types of bilateral stimulation do you offer?
- How do you handle it when current responsibilities become overwhelming?
- How do you know when to slow down or shift away from memory work?
- How might EMDR and IFS be used differently in our conversations?

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to talk continuously while doing bilateral stimulation?

No, continuous talking is not required during bilateral stimulation. A therapist may invite a few words about what you notice after each short set, and you can share as much or as little detail as is workable. Conversation remains available throughout the process.

### Can EMDR include physical sensations?

Yes, physical sensations can be part of what is noticed in EMDR. A therapist may ask where a sensation is located or whether it changes as attention shifts. This is observation, not a requirement to interpret every bodily response.

### What if I cannot settle on one memory to work with?

You do not need to settle on one memory immediately. A therapist can begin with a current concern, a recurring reaction, or a broad theme and use conversation to identify possible connections. The focus can remain tentative and can change as more context becomes clear.

### Are breaks allowed during EMDR sessions?

Yes, breaks can be part of an EMDR session. You can ask to pause, get water, orient to the room, or discuss what is happening. The therapist can also suggest a pause when the pace needs adjustment.

### What should I listen for when a therapist explains EMDR?

Listen for a clear explanation of consent, pacing, choice, and how the therapist responds when difficult material arises. It is reasonable to ask how sessions are structured and how they decide whether to use bilateral stimulation. You can also notice whether the explanation makes room for your current responsibilities and limits.
