# What to expect in a first EMDR therapy session

*By Lena Park, LMFT 778899*

Knowing what to expect in a first EMDR therapy session can make it easier to decide whether the approach and therapist feel like a workable fit. A first appointment is generally a conversation, not a test and not a requirement to begin working on a particular memory right away. Lena Park, LMFT, uses a careful, collaborative approach that makes room for the part of you that is always on duty, whether life is shaped by a demanding career, new parenthood, or both. The appointment is a chance to understand what has brought you in, explain how the work may be structured, and decide together what makes sense to address. It can also give you a sense of the pace and communication involved.

## Will I work on a difficult memory during the first appointment?

Usually, a first appointment does not require beginning focused memory work. The initial meeting is often used to get oriented, discuss what has been happening, and establish whether EMDR therapy is an approach you want to consider.

Some people arrive with a clear event, image, or period of life on their mind. Others know that certain situations, sensations, emotions, or patterns keep pulling at their attention but are not sure how to name the starting point. Both are useful places to begin a conversation. You do not need to organize your life story before you arrive.

A therapist may ask broad questions about the concerns that led you to seek therapy, what feels most present now, and what you would like therapy to make room for. The purpose is not to collect every detail at once. It is to develop an initial shared picture of the concerns, your context, and the way you prefer to approach difficult material.

## The purpose of a first EMDR appointment

The purpose of a first EMDR appointment is to decide how to proceed thoughtfully, rather than to rush into a set procedure. EMDR is a structured therapy approach, and the early conversations help a therapist understand whether its structure fits the concerns you want to bring in.

Lena Park Therapy approaches this work collaboratively. That means the therapist can explain the parts of the approach, invite questions, and check what information or language feels useful to you. Therapy may include attention to the practical demands that take up your day as well as experiences that have stayed emotionally charged or hard to set aside.

An initial appointment can also clarify what you want from the therapeutic relationship. For someone who is used to managing everyone else's needs, even identifying what would make a conversation feel manageable may be important information. You can talk about preferences, concerns, boundaries, and anything that would affect how you participate in therapy.

## What information will the therapist ask about?

The therapist will usually ask about your current concerns, relevant background, and the circumstances of your life now. Questions may cover changes in mood or stress, relationships, work or caregiving demands, prior therapy experiences, health factors that may matter to treatment, and the kinds of situations that tend to feel especially difficult.

The conversation may also include questions about how you respond when you are overwhelmed, disconnected, highly alert, shut down, or pulled into old reactions. These questions are not meant to label you. They help the therapist understand what support, structure, and communication may be important before deciding how to organize the work.

It can be useful to mention what has and has not worked for you in past therapy, including anything that felt too fast, too vague, or difficult to talk about. You can also name practical realities that affect your bandwidth, such as a demanding role at work, interrupted sleep, caregiving, or a schedule that leaves little room to pause. These details help put your concerns in context.

## How are consent and choice handled in the first session?

Consent and choice should be part of the first session from the start. A therapist can describe the proposed process, answer questions in ordinary language, and check whether you want to continue with a particular topic or exercise.

You remain allowed to say that something is unclear, that you would prefer to stay with a broader overview, or that you do not want to discuss a subject in that appointment. Therapy is a collaborative conversation, and your feedback gives the therapist information about how to work together. Agreement to attend an appointment is not agreement to discuss every topic that may be relevant.

The first meeting is also a place to discuss how you tend to communicate when something does not feel right. Some people readily speak up, while others notice themselves agreeing, explaining, or taking care of the other person. Naming that pattern can be useful, especially in a practice that makes room for the part of you that keeps everything running.

## How can I prepare for a first EMDR therapy session?

You can prepare simply by bringing a general sense of what you want to discuss and any questions you want answered. There is no need to create a polished account of your history or decide in advance which experiences matter most. A few notes can be helpful if you tend to lose track of what you wanted to mention once a conversation begins.

Consider what you want to know about the therapist's way of working. You might ask how early sessions are structured, how the therapist handles uncertainty or strong emotions, how decisions about next steps are made, and how you can share feedback if the process is not working for you. These are practical questions about the working relationship, not signs that you need to have everything figured out.

At the end of an initial appointment, the therapist may summarize what they heard and discuss possible directions for future work. You can use that conversation to consider whether the approach, communication style, and level of collaboration fit what you are looking for. It is also appropriate to take time to think about whether you want to continue.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I bring notes to my first appointment?

Yes, bringing notes is completely appropriate. You might write down a few concerns, patterns you have noticed, relevant questions, or past therapy experiences you want the therapist to know about. The notes do not need to be detailed or organized in any particular way.

### Do I need to know my complete history before I start?

No, you do not need a complete or chronological account of your history. A first appointment can begin with what feels most important or most present now. Background can be discussed gradually as it becomes relevant to the work.

### Can I decide not to begin EMDR after the first meeting?

Yes, an initial appointment is an opportunity to gather information and consider fit. You can decide that you want more conversation before considering EMDR, that another focus makes more sense, or that you do not want to continue. It is appropriate to share that decision directly.

### What if I get emotional while talking during the appointment?

Emotion can come up when discussing difficult experiences or ongoing pressures. You can tell the therapist what you are noticing, ask for clarification, or ask to shift the conversation. The therapist can use that information when deciding how to continue the appointment.

### Is it okay if I do not know what I want from therapy yet?

Yes, uncertainty is a valid place to begin. You may know that something is taking a toll without having a precise goal or explanation for it. The first conversation can help put language around what you want to explore and what kind of support you are seeking.
