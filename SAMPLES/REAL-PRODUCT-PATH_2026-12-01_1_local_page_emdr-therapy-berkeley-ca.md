## What is EMDR therapy?

EMDR, or Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing, is a therapy approach that helps a person work with distressing memories, beliefs, body sensations, and emotional responses in a structured way.

Rather than asking you to give a detailed account of every experience, EMDR uses focused attention on a selected memory or current concern while adding bilateral stimulation. Bilateral stimulation simply means alternating input from one side of the body to the other, which may involve eye movements, tapping, or sound.

EMDR includes preparation and pacing, not only memory-focused work. The process considers what is happening in the present, what from the past may still be activated, and how you want to respond in situations that matter now. Lena Park Therapy makes room for the part of you that is always on duty, whether you are navigating new parenthood, a demanding career, or both.

## Who may consider EMDR therapy?

EMDR therapy may be worth considering when past experiences continue to show up in thoughts, emotions, body responses, relationships, or situations that seem difficult to explain.

People seek this approach for many reasons, including a single upsetting event, repeated stressful experiences, grief, conflict, loss, or patterns of self-criticism and over-responsibility. It can also be relevant when a person is functioning capably on the outside while carrying a great deal internally.

A conversation with a therapist can help clarify whether EMDR fits the concerns you want to bring to therapy and whether preparation should come first. EMDR is not a one-size-fits-all process; the focus, pace, and amount of time spent on stabilization are part of the clinical conversation.

## What happens in an EMDR session?

An EMDR session usually begins with conversation, shared planning, and attention to what feels manageable to address that day.

Early sessions commonly include discussing your history at a level that feels appropriate, identifying current concerns, and developing ways to stay oriented during difficult material. This can include noticing physical cues, practicing grounding, and identifying resources or supports that help you return attention to the present.

When it is appropriate to focus on a target, the therapist may invite you to notice an image, thought, emotion, or body sensation connected to it. You would then use an agreed form of bilateral stimulation for brief sets, pausing between sets to notice what is coming up. You remain able to speak, slow down, ask questions, or decide not to continue with a particular focus.

Sessions also include time to close the work thoughtfully. The goal is not to push through material or to produce a particular reaction; it is to maintain collaboration and attention to what is happening in the room.

## How do EMDR and IFS work together?

EMDR and IFS can be used together when it is useful to understand the different parts of a person’s inner experience before or alongside memory-focused work.

IFS, or Internal Family Systems, is a way of noticing that people may have different inner responses with different jobs: a part that organizes, a part that worries, a part that shuts down, or a part that keeps moving no matter what. This language is not meant to suggest that anything is wrong with you. It can offer a practical way to describe internal conflict and competing needs.

In Lena Park’s approach, attention to these parts can support careful pacing in EMDR. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to talk through every detail of a difficult experience?

No, EMDR does not require you to give a full verbal account of every detail. You and the therapist can decide what information is useful for understanding the concern and selecting a focus. The amount you share is part of the ongoing collaborative process.

### What is bilateral stimulation?

Bilateral stimulation is alternating input from the left and right sides of the body. In EMDR, it may involve eye movements, tapping, or alternating sounds. A therapist can explain the options and discuss what feels workable for you.

### Can EMDR include current stress, not only the past?

Yes, EMDR can include present-day situations, reactions, and beliefs as part of the overall focus. Past experiences may be discussed when they appear relevant to what is happening now. The direction of the work is decided together.

### What if I am not sure which memory to focus on?

You do not need to identify a single memory before contacting a therapist. Therapy can begin by discussing current patterns, concerns, and situations that stand out. Those conversations can help identify possible areas for attention.

### Is it okay to ask for a slower pace?

Yes, pacing is an important part of EMDR therapy. You can raise concerns, ask questions, pause, or discuss changing focus at any point. A collaborative approach includes attending to what feels manageable in the session.
