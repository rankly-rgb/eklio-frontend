## Why consider therapy when your career is going well?

Therapy can be relevant even when work is going well on paper. Professional competence, responsibility, and ambition can coexist with a persistent sense of being on call, difficulty pausing, strong reactions to mistakes, or uncertainty about what remains when productivity is not required.

The focus does not have to be a crisis or a single problem. Sessions can provide time to look at recurring patterns: how you respond to demands, what happens around rest or delegation, how feedback lands, or why certain situations carry more weight than expected. For new parents with demanding careers, these conversations can also include the pull between caregiving, work, partnership, and personal expectations.

Lena Park Therapy approaches these topics without assuming ambition is a problem to be solved. Instead, therapy makes room for the part of you that is always on duty and considers what that part may be trying to manage.

## What concerns can professional therapy address?

Therapy for professionals can address the emotional and relational dimensions of a demanding life, including concerns that may not have a neat label. You can bring in a current situation, a repeated workplace pattern, a transition at home, or a reaction that seems disproportionate to the moment.

The conversation may include topics such as:

- Pressure to perform, produce, or remain dependable
- Difficulty setting limits when others rely on you
- Perfectionism, self-criticism, or fear of making an error
- Reactions to feedback, conflict, or shifting expectations
- The impact of past experiences that still show up in present situations
- Tension between professional identity, family responsibilities, and rest

## How can EMDR and IFS be part of this work?

EMDR and IFS can be used as thoughtful ways of exploring present-day reactions, past experiences, and the protective strategies that keep a person moving. Neither approach needs to define every session, and the focus is decided collaboratively based on what seems important to discuss.

EMDR is a structured therapy approach that may be used to work with experiences, images, beliefs, body sensations, or present situations connected to a concern. IFS, or Internal Family Systems, uses the idea of “parts” as a practical way to notice different inner responses—for example, the part that pushes for excellence, the part that anticipates problems, or the part that wants to withdraw. You do not need to adopt any particular belief system for this language to be useful.

In this practice, these approaches are used carefully and with ongoing attention to choice. A session may involve ordinary conversation, noticing what arises around a specific situation, or deciding together whether a structured exercise fits the moment.

## What is different about therapy for people who carry a lot?

Therapy for people who carry a lot pays attention to the fact that competence can function as both a strength and a demanding role. When others count on you, it may be easy to explain a concern away, turn it into a task, or stay focused on the next responsibility rather than pausing with your own experience.

Lena Park’s approach is careful and collaborative. Rather than treating a highly organized or high-functioning presentation as evidence that nothing needs attention, the work can explore how responsibility, vigilance, achievement, and self-expectation operate in daily life. The conversation can hold practical realities alongside questions about the internal rules that make slowing down, asking for support, or tolerating uncertainty complicated.

New parenthood can add another layer to an already demanding professional role. Sessions can make room for the part of you that is always on duty, whether that pressure appears in caregiving, career decisions, partnership, or the effort to keep multiple areas of life moving.

## How do I choose a therapist for work-related pressure?

Choose a therapist whose approach, communication style, and way of discussing your concerns feel clear enough to evaluate. It can be useful to ask how the therapist thinks about achievement, workplace stress, privacy, past experiences, and the relationship between practical problems and emotional patterns.

You may also ask how EMDR or IFS would be introduced, how you would decide together what to focus on, and what happens if a particular approach does not fit. These questions are not a test of whether you have the right reason for therapy; they are part of deciding whether the working relationship and approach make sense for you.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to be burned out to seek therapy?

No. Therapy can begin with a concern, pattern, transition, or question that matters to you, whether or not you use the word burnout. You might want to discuss ongoing pressure, a work situation, family demands, or the ways responsibility shapes your daily decisions.

### Can we talk about career decisions without making therapy into coaching?

Yes. Career decisions can be part of therapy while the conversation also attends to the emotions, expectations, relationships, and past experiences connected to those decisions. The focus is not on giving you a prescribed answer, but on exploring what is involved for you.

### What if I am more comfortable discussing facts than feelings?

That is a workable starting point. Sessions can begin with concrete situations, details of a recent interaction, work responsibilities, or the thoughts that have been occupying your attention. Emotional material does not need to be forced or performed.

### Can therapy include the impact of parenting on my work life?

Yes. Parenting and professional life can be discussed together, including conflicting responsibilities, changes in identity, partnership concerns, and pressure to keep functioning across multiple roles. The conversation can stay connected to the practical realities you are navigating.
