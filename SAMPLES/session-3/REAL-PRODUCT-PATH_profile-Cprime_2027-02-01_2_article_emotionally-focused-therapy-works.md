# How emotionally focused therapy works in couples therapy

*By Diego Ramirez, Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist, LMFT 119034*

Understanding how emotionally focused therapy works can make couples counseling seem less mysterious. Emotionally focused therapy, often called EFT, is a structured approach that looks closely at the repeated interaction patterns between partners and the emotions that shape those patterns. Rather than treating one person as the problem, the work examines what happens between two people when a difficult moment unfolds.

## What does EFT pay attention to in a difficult moment?

EFT pays attention to the sequence of reactions that unfolds between partners during a difficult moment. This can include what one person says or does, how the other person interprets it, and the response that follows. A sharp comment, a request for reassurance, a change of subject, silence, or leaving the room can all become part of a familiar sequence.

The therapist listens for the immediate interaction as well as the experience underneath it. For example, criticism may be connected to frustration, worry, loneliness, or a wish to matter. Pulling back may be connected to feeling overwhelmed, uncertain how to respond, or fearing that speaking will make the situation worse. These possibilities are explored rather than assumed.

This focus does not mean practical concerns are ignored. Partners may bring up money, household work, parenting, family boundaries, intimacy, or differing priorities. EFT considers how the conversation about the topic develops: what happens when the subject is raised, how each partner responds, and where the exchange begins to become stuck.

## How does emotionally focused therapy slow down conflict?

Emotionally focused therapy slows down conflict by examining one part of an interaction at a time. In everyday life, a disagreement can move quickly from a small comment to raised voices, withdrawal, defensiveness, or repeating points neither person feels have been understood. In session, the therapist may pause the exchange and ask about what happened just before a reaction occurred.

Slowing down is not the same as deciding who is right. It is a way to make room for details that can disappear during an argument. A therapist may ask what a remark meant to a partner, what they noticed in their body, what they wanted to say but did not say, or what made it hard to stay engaged. This can clarify the difference between an intention and the impact of a response.

The therapist may also put the pattern into plain language. One partner may press for a response as tension rises, while the other becomes quieter or more distant; the increased distance may then lead to more pressing. Naming this sequence allows the couple to look at it together instead of treating it as evidence that one partner is entirely at fault.

## What are attachment needs in EFT?

Attachment needs in EFT are the basic relationship concerns that can become especially important when partners feel disconnected, uncertain, or under strain. They may include wanting to know that a partner is available, wanting to be taken seriously, wanting closeness, or wanting room to speak without being immediately dismissed or met with escalation.

These needs are not presented as demands that one person must perfectly fulfill. They provide context for reactions that may otherwise look confusing or contradictory. Someone may argue intensely because the relationship feels distant to them, while someone else may become quiet because closeness during conflict feels risky or overwhelming. EFT explores the meaning of these reactions within the relationship.

A therapist may invite each partner to describe the more vulnerable part of their experience in language that is specific and manageable. This is different from requiring partners to disclose everything at once or to use a particular emotional vocabulary. The pace of the conversation is part of the work, particularly when a topic brings up strong reactions.

## What role does the therapist take in EFT?

The therapist takes an active role in guiding the conversation, tracking the interaction, and helping partners stay with the subject at hand. EFT is not simply a place where each person gives their version of events while the therapist listens from the sidelines. The therapist may interrupt a familiar exchange, ask a question that narrows the focus, or reflect a pattern that has just appeared in the room.

At Ramirez Couples Therapy, the approach is direct and structured for partners who may feel more like roommates than a team. The structure involves returning to the interaction that is happening between partners and identifying the moves each person makes when connection feels uncertain. It can also include clear attention to how a conversation begins, where it changes direction, and what each partner is trying to protect or communicate.

The therapist does not act as a judge, choose a winning account, or require partners to agree about every detail of the past. When memories differ, the work can focus on how each person experienced an event and how those experiences entered the relationship. Accountability can still be discussed directly when words or actions have had an impact.

## How are EFT conversations structured over time?

EFT conversations are structured around identifying a recurring pattern, exploring the emotions and meanings within it, and practicing different ways of responding in the session. The exact topics may shift from meeting to meeting, but the therapist continues to connect them to the relationship pattern under discussion. A disagreement about plans, a tense reunion after work, or an argument about responsibilities may all reveal similar interactional moves.

Early conversations commonly include learning how each partner describes the concern and observing how the pair communicates when the concern is discussed. The therapist may ask about recent examples rather than attempting to cover every disagreement the relationship has ever had. Specific examples provide material for tracking the sequence in detail.

As the work continues, the therapist may invite a partner to speak more directly to the other about an underlying concern, while supporting the receiving partner in responding to what was actually said. These are guided conversations, not performances. If the exchange becomes too intense, confusing, or shut down, the therapist can slow it down, clarify what is happening, and return to a smaller part of the interaction.

## What should partners ask before starting EFT?

Partners can ask how the therapist explains EFT, how sessions are guided when conflict rises, and how practical concerns fit into the work. These questions can clarify whether the therapist’s style feels sufficiently structured and direct for the concerns being brought in. It can also be useful to ask how the therapist handles situations where one person speaks more quickly, needs more time, or has a different understanding of the relationship problem.

It is reasonable to ask what the therapist expects from both partners during conversations. For example, partners may want to know whether they will be invited to speak directly to each other, whether the therapist will pause an escalating exchange, and how the therapist approaches sensitive subjects such as intimacy, trust, family relationships, or major decisions.

A consultation with a couples therapist can also be a time to describe any circumstances that may affect the work. This might include ongoing high-conflict interactions, uncertainty about continuing the relationship, substance use, concerns about safety, or individual experiences that make certain discussions difficult. Sharing relevant context allows the therapist to consider what kind of structure is appropriate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is EFT mainly about talking more openly?

No, EFT is not simply an invitation to talk more or share more intensely. It focuses on the interaction between partners, including how each person responds when the conversation becomes emotionally charged. Openness may be part of the work, but it is guided and connected to the pattern being explored.

### What if we remember an argument differently?

Different memories do not prevent EFT from being used. The therapist can explore what the event meant to each partner and how each person reacted, without requiring a single agreed-upon account. The conversation can also address the impact of words or actions even when details are disputed.

### Do partners need to use emotional language outside of therapy?

No, partners are not required to speak in a particular therapeutic style outside sessions. In therapy, the clinician may help translate broad reactions such as anger, shutdown, or frustration into more specific experiences. Partners can then consider what language is workable and natural for them.

### Can EFT include a conversation about a breach of trust?

Yes, EFT can include conversations about a breach of trust when that issue is part of the relationship concern. The therapist may structure the discussion carefully, attend to the impact on each partner, and monitor whether the conversation remains workable. The details and pace depend on what is being discussed.

### What if conflict becomes too intense during a session?

The therapist can pause the exchange and narrow the focus to what is happening in that moment. This may involve identifying a recent trigger, asking one partner to speak more slowly, or helping the other describe their reaction before continuing. A pause is part of the structure of the conversation, not a sign that the subject cannot be discussed.
