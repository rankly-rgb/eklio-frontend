Therapy for caregivers in Oakland, CA offers a dedicated time to discuss the demands of caring for others alongside experiences from the past that may still carry weight. ## Caregiver therapy at Ellis Counseling

Caregiver therapy at Ellis Counseling centers the practical and emotional realities of regularly caring for other people. The conversation can include the responsibilities that fill your days, the roles you hold in your family or community, and older experiences that may come up alongside present demands.

Caregiving can involve many kinds of attention: coordinating needs, responding to crises, making decisions, anticipating what someone else may require, or staying available when plans change. Therapy does not require those responsibilities to be set aside before they can be discussed. They can provide important context for deciding what deserves attention in a session.

Jordan Ellis’s approach draws on EMDR and Internal Family Systems, often called IFS. These are structured therapy approaches that can be discussed in ordinary language, with attention to choice, pacing, and what is manageable within a life that does not pause.

## Why consider therapy while caring for others?

Therapy can offer a regular setting for looking at caregiving demands and the experiences that may be difficult to hold alone. Caregivers may spend substantial time responding to another person’s needs while having few places to consider their own reactions, limits, memories, or competing responsibilities.

The focus does not have to be a single crisis or one clearly defined event. A session may concern a current caregiving decision, a recurring reaction, an old experience that feels relevant now, or the strain of carrying several concerns at once. It is also possible to begin without having a polished explanation for why you are seeking therapy.

The work can make room for topics such as:

- The ongoing responsibilities involved in caring for another person
- Memories or experiences that arise in the context of present caregiving
- Conflicting thoughts, emotions, or internal pressures
- Family roles, boundaries, and decisions
- The difficulty of having little uninterrupted time for yourself

## EMDR for caregivers

EMDR is a therapy approach that uses careful attention to experiences, present reactions, and bilateral stimulation when that is part of the work. Bilateral stimulation refers to alternating input, such as eye movements, sounds, or tapping, used within a structured EMDR process.

For a caregiver, EMDR may include discussion of an older event, a period of sustained responsibility, something witnessed while caring for another person, or a present situation that brings up strong associations. It does not require a perfect memory or a detailed account of every part of an experience. The pace and focus are discussed collaboratively rather than assumed.

At Ellis Counseling, EMDR is approached patiently around the fact that caregiving obligations may remain active. Time may be spent orienting to what is happening now, identifying what feels important to address, and considering whether a particular focus is appropriate for that day.

## A patient pace for a life that does not pause

A patient pace means therapy is not treated as a race toward a particular disclosure, memory, or conclusion. Caregiving can involve changing needs and limited bandwidth, so the work can account for what is currently on your plate rather than insisting that every session follow the same intensity or direction.

Pacing may involve spending time on present-day concerns, slowing down when a topic feels like too much to take on that day, or choosing to stay with a smaller piece of a larger concern. It can also include naming practical limits, such as what feels possible to discuss given the rest of the day’s responsibilities.

You remain part of decisions about the focus and pace of sessions. Questions, preferences, uncertainty, and requests to pause or shift attention are relevant information in therapy, not interruptions to it.

## What is the role of IFS in caregiver therapy?

IFS is an approach that helps people notice different inner responses without treating those responses as a problem to argue away. In everyday terms, someone may recognize one side that stays organized and responsible, another that feels overwhelmed, another that pushes for control, or another that wants distance from a difficult topic.

For caregivers, these responses can be especially relevant when many demands compete for attention. IFS provides a way to slow down and become curious about these reactions, what they may be trying to do, and how they show up in current caregiving situations or in connection with older experiences.

IFS and EMDR may each be used when relevant, but there is no requirement to use both in every session. The approach can be discussed openly, including what each method involves and what questions you have before deciding how to proceed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is caregiver therapy only for people caring for a family member?

No. Caregiving can describe many forms of ongoing responsibility for another person. The conversation can begin with the specific roles and circumstances that are most relevant in your life.

### Can I bring up practical responsibilities as well as emotional concerns?

Yes. Practical responsibilities are often part of the context for therapy, especially when they shape what is possible day to day. They can be discussed alongside thoughts, emotions, memories, and relationship concerns.

### Must I choose between EMDR and IFS before contacting the practice?

No. You can ask questions about both approaches before deciding whether either is appropriate to explore. The initial conversation can include what brings you in and how each approach may be used.

### What if caregiving demands change from week to week?

Changing demands can be part of the therapy conversation. Session focus and pace can be revisited as current responsibilities, decisions, or stressors shift.
