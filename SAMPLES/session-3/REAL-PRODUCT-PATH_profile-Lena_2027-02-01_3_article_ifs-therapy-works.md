# How IFS Therapy Works

*By Lena Park, Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist, LMFT 778899*

Understanding how IFS therapy works can make the approach less mysterious before you decide whether its language and structure fit what you want to discuss. Internal Family Systems, usually called IFS, is a psychotherapy approach that looks at the different inner responses that can show up around pressure, conflict, memories, caregiving, work, and daily decisions. Rather than treating those responses as problems to argue with or suppress, IFS approaches them with curiosity and care.

## What is the basic idea behind IFS therapy?

IFS therapy starts with the idea that people can have different inner parts with different jobs, concerns, and ways of responding. A part might push for productivity, monitor for mistakes, avoid a difficult conversation, become critical, or carry strong sadness, fear, or anger. Calling these responses “parts” is a practical way to notice a pattern without reducing a person to that pattern.

The approach also uses the term Self to describe a grounded, curious, and connected inner capacity. Self is not another part that has to perform correctly, and it is not a claim that someone has one permanent emotional state. In IFS, therapy makes room to notice whether there is enough steadiness and curiosity available to relate to a part rather than becoming completely caught up in its reaction.

This framework can be particularly relevant when someone appears capable on the outside but experiences competing demands internally. For example, one part may insist on staying organized and useful while another protests the pace, withdraws, or reacts sharply. IFS does not assume either response is wrong; it asks what each one may be trying to manage.

## Why does IFS describe inner responses as parts?

IFS uses the word parts because it can make complicated inner conflict easier to observe and discuss. Many people already use everyday versions of this language, such as “part of me wants to do this, but another part does not.” In therapy, that observation becomes a starting point for looking more closely at what happens before, during, and after a reaction.

A part is not a separate person, and IFS does not require someone to think of themselves as fragmented. It is a model for paying attention to recurring protective strategies, emotional states, beliefs, and impulses. The language can be adapted: someone might prefer to call them patterns, sides, reactions, or modes. What matters is whether naming a response creates enough distance to examine it.

Parts are often discussed in broad groupings. Protective parts may try to prevent vulnerability by planning, pleasing, controlling, achieving, numbing, distracting, or staying busy. Other parts may carry emotions or experiences that feel especially difficult to approach. These are descriptions of roles within the model, not labels a therapist assigns as a verdict.

- A driven part may focus on output, preparation, or avoiding errors
- A watchful part may scan conversations for criticism or conflict
- A protective part may use distance, distraction, or shutdown when something feels too intense
- A younger-feeling part may carry fear, grief, shame, or loneliness connected to earlier experiences

## How does an IFS therapist work with a protective part?

An IFS therapist usually begins by helping you notice a protective part’s concerns and role before asking it to step back. This may involve identifying when the response appears, what it says, what it fears could happen, and what it is trying to prevent. The point is not to force the reaction to disappear or to persuade it that it is irrational.

The therapist may invite you to observe the part with some curiosity. If irritation, fear, embarrassment, or urgency toward that part is strong, the work can pause there. Those reactions may also be understood as parts with something important to say. This is sometimes called unblending: creating a little room between you and the response so it can be noticed rather than directing the entire conversation.

Permission and pace matter in this process. A protective part may not want to discuss certain material, may prefer to stay close to practical concerns, or may signal that more information is needed before going further. In careful IFS work, that hesitation is treated as relevant information, not as resistance to overcome.

## What is it like to explore a difficult feeling in IFS?

Exploring a difficult feeling in IFS involves approaching it gradually and checking what feels manageable in the moment. A therapist may ask where you notice the feeling, what images or words come with it, how old it seems, or what it wants you to understand. You can answer directly, speak tentatively, or say that you do not know.

At times, a present-day situation may lead to memories, body sensations, or meanings connected to earlier experiences. IFS does not require a detailed retelling of every event. The work can remain with what is currently noticeable, and the therapist can return attention to the present when needed. If a response feels too intense, slowing down, orienting to the room, or shifting to another topic may be appropriate.

The goal of these questions is to understand the relationship among inner responses, not to produce a particular emotional display. Some sessions may center on words and reflection; others may use imagination, noticing physical sensations, or an internal dialogue. Lena Park Therapy approaches this work carefully and collaboratively, making room for the part that stays on duty amid the demands of parenting, work, or both.

## What does a typical IFS conversation focus on?

An IFS conversation can focus on a current reaction, an ongoing pattern, or a conflict between competing needs. A starting point might be a tense exchange, a cycle of overpreparing, difficulty resting, an emotional response that seems larger than the immediate situation, or uncertainty about a decision. You do not need to arrive with the “right” part identified.

The therapist generally follows the thread that seems most relevant while checking in about your experience. Questions may explore what a part wants, what it worries about, what triggers it, and how it relates to other parts. Practical context remains important: family responsibilities, workplace expectations, sleep, relationships, and current stress can all shape how a pattern functions.

IFS is not a script that has to be followed in the same way every time. Some conversations may be largely practical, especially when daily demands need attention. At other points, the work may become more inwardly focused. A collaborative approach leaves room to discuss what feels useful, what does not fit, and whether the language of parts is helping you think more clearly.

## What should I ask when considering IFS therapy?

Useful questions about IFS therapy address the therapist’s approach to pace, consent, and how they work when strong reactions arise. A conversation with a prospective therapist can clarify how they explain the model, whether they adapt its language, and how they handle concerns about going too deeply or too quickly.

It can also be useful to ask how therapy will make room for the realities that brought you in. Adults who hold a great deal together may need a framework that can include both high-functioning routines and the costs of maintaining them. At Lena Park Therapy, IFS may be part of a broader conversation about the patterns that keep someone constantly on duty.

You are allowed to assess the fit of the approach and the working relationship. Therapy is a collaborative process, and questions about structure, boundaries, and what you want to focus on belong in that process.

- How do you explain IFS without relying on specialized language?
- How do you decide whether to slow down or shift focus?
- What happens if I do not connect with the idea of parts?
- How do you include practical pressures alongside emotional material?
- How do you approach memories or feelings that feel difficult to discuss?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is IFS the same as having multiple personalities?

No. IFS uses “parts” as a way to describe familiar inner reactions, conflicts, and protective patterns. It does not assume that a person has separate identities or require any particular diagnosis.

### Do I have to close my eyes or use visualization in IFS?

No. Some IFS exercises may involve imagery or noticing inwardly, but these are not requirements. You can discuss what you notice in words, keep your eyes open, or ask to use a different way of working.

### What if one inner reaction feels stronger than all the others?

A strong reaction can be the focus of the conversation, without assuming it tells the whole story. The therapist may help you notice its triggers, concerns, and impact while moving at a pace that is manageable.

### Can IFS be combined with EMDR?

It can be, depending on the focus of therapy and the clinical approach being used. IFS and EMDR are distinct methods, and a therapist can discuss how each might fit into the work without assuming both are needed.

### Do I need to use the word Self in IFS therapy?

No. Self is a term used within the IFS model, but the language should be understandable and workable for you. A therapist can explain the idea in plain language and discuss whether it is useful in your conversations.
