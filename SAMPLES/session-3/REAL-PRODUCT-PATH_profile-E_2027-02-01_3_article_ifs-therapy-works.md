# How IFS therapy works: understanding the process

*By Jordan Ellis, Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist, LMFT 121877*

Understanding how IFS therapy works can make an unfamiliar approach easier to consider. Internal Family Systems, commonly called IFS, is a form of psychotherapy that uses the idea that people can have different inner reactions, perspectives, and protective strategies at the same time. Rather than treating those reactions as defects or asking someone to argue with them, IFS approaches them with curiosity. At Ellis Counseling, Jordan Ellis, Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist, uses patient IFS and EMDR with adults who spend much of their day caring for others and rarely get to put the old thing down.

## The central idea behind IFS

IFS understands the mind as containing different “parts,” along with a steadier capacity for awareness called Self. A part is not a separate person or personality. It is a useful way to describe a recognizable inner response: the part that plans for every possibility, pushes through exhaustion, becomes sharply self-critical, avoids a difficult topic, or wants everyone else to be okay first.

The model also uses the word Self for a state of calm, curiosity, clarity, and connection that may be more available at some times than others. In IFS, Self is not something a therapist gives a person, and it is not a demand to stay calm. It is language for the observing, responsive stance that can notice an inner reaction without immediately being run by it.

These ideas can be especially relevant when someone has long been responsible for other people’s needs. A highly organized, watchful, or self-sacrificing response may have important reasons for being there. IFS makes room to understand what that response is trying to manage rather than reducing it to a problem. The work can include current responsibilities, relationships, emotions, physical sensations, and experiences from earlier in life.

## How does IFS explore a strong reaction?

IFS explores a strong reaction by slowing down enough to notice it, describe it, and become curious about its role. The starting point might be a present-day moment: irritation after a request, dread before a conversation, a harsh inner comment, difficulty resting, or an urge to stay busy. The therapist may invite attention to what happens internally when that moment comes to mind.

Instead of asking whether the reaction is logical, IFS asks questions about its function. What does it want to accomplish? What is it concerned could happen if it did not do its job? How old does it seem, if it has a sense of age? Where is it noticed in the body? These are invitations, not tests. It is also fine if answers are uncertain, incomplete, or not available in words.

A person may notice more than one response at once. For example, one part may want to talk about a situation while another wants to change the subject. IFS treats this conflict as meaningful information. The therapist helps the conversation proceed in a way that acknowledges both reactions, rather than requiring one of them to disappear before anything can be discussed.

## What are protectors and vulnerable parts in IFS?

IFS uses the terms protectors and vulnerable parts to describe different roles inner responses can take. Protectors are responses that try to prevent pain, embarrassment, conflict, overwhelm, or other experiences they view as risky. They may show up through overworking, people-pleasing, emotional distance, perfectionism, criticism, distraction, or efforts to remain in control.

IFS commonly distinguishes between protective responses that work ahead of time to prevent a feared situation and those that react quickly when distress is already present. These categories are not labels for a person. They are simply ways to understand the timing and purpose of a response. A strategy that causes difficulty now may still be organized around protection.

Vulnerable parts may hold emotions, beliefs, or memories that feel tender, frightening, lonely, ashamed, or overwhelmed. IFS does not require immediate access to these experiences. When protectors signal concern, that concern is part of the work. A patient pace matters, particularly for adults balancing caregiving, work, family demands, or ongoing stressors.

## The role of the therapist in IFS

An IFS therapist guides attention, asks careful questions, and helps keep the conversation collaborative and paced. The therapist may invite you to notice a part, check how you feel toward it, and see whether there is enough internal distance to approach it with curiosity. If the answer is no, the work may focus on the reaction that is closest to the surface instead.

The therapist does not decide what a part means or insist on a particular image, memory, or explanation. Some people experience parts as thoughts, moods, body sensations, or shifts in posture. Others use images, names, or a sense of age. Ordinary language is enough. Saying “the part of me that cannot stop planning” can be a complete starting point.

At Ellis Counseling, IFS can be approached with attention to the demands that do not pause outside the therapy room. Jordan Ellis may ask about what is happening in daily life, what feels manageable to discuss, and what needs to remain in the foreground. Therapy can include both the old material that still shows up and the responsibilities that require attention now.

## What can someone expect between IFS sessions?

Between IFS sessions, someone may simply notice inner reactions with a little more specificity, without needing to analyze or change them. A therapist might suggest paying attention to when a familiar response appears, what seems to prompt it, and how it is experienced in thoughts, emotions, or the body. There is no requirement to keep a journal, use formal exercises, or work on difficult material alone.

It can be useful to recognize that daily life may not offer much uninterrupted time for reflection, especially for people caring for others. Noticing can happen briefly: in the car, after a phone call, while preparing for the next task, or before sleep. If something feels too intense, it is appropriate to step back from examining it and bring that information into the next conversation.

Questions to keep in mind between appointments can include

- What response showed up most strongly today?
- What seemed to set it in motion?
- What might that response have been trying to handle?
- Was there another reaction present at the same time?
- What helped me notice without pushing for an answer?

## How to decide whether IFS is worth discussing with a therapist

IFS may be worth discussing if you want a structured way to explore conflicting reactions without treating one side of you as the enemy. The approach can be relevant when a person notices recurring patterns around responsibility, closeness, control, avoidance, self-criticism, or responding to stress. It can also be discussed when the connection between a current reaction and the past is unclear.

A consultation is a chance to ask how a therapist explains the approach, how they handle moments of uncertainty or intensity, and how current life demands are incorporated. You can also ask whether the therapist uses IFS on its own or alongside other approaches, such as EMDR, and how they decide what to focus on in a given conversation.

The aim of these questions is not to find perfect answers before therapy begins. It is to understand the therapist’s process and determine whether the language and pace make sense for what you want to talk about. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can IFS be useful if I tend to intellectualize everything?

Yes, IFS can include thinking and analysis while also inviting attention to emotions, body cues, and protective responses. An intellectual response may itself be approached with curiosity rather than treated as an obstacle. The therapist can work with what is available in the moment.

### What if I feel skeptical about talking to inner parts?

Skepticism can be discussed openly in IFS. Parts language is a framework, not a requirement to believe that different personalities exist inside you. A therapist can use everyday descriptions of reactions, habits, and competing impulses.

### Can IFS be used when stress is ongoing?

Yes, IFS conversations can include stressors that are still happening. The work may focus on the reactions that arise around current demands and on what feels realistic to explore. Ongoing responsibilities can remain part of the context rather than being set aside.

### Do I need to share every detail for IFS to work?

No, you can set limits on what you discuss. IFS can begin with a current feeling, thought, body sensation, or pattern without recounting every detail of an experience. Decisions about depth and focus can be revisited during therapy.

### Is IFS the same as having multiple personalities?

No, IFS uses “parts” as a way of talking about common inner complexity, such as competing feelings or protective habits. It does not assume that a person has separate personalities. A therapist can explain the model in language that fits your understanding.
