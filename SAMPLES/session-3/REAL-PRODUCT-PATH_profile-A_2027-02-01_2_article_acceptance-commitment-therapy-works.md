# How acceptance and commitment therapy works

*By Ines Alvarez, Licensed Psychologist, PSY 28456*

Understanding how acceptance and commitment therapy works can make its language feel more concrete before you decide whether the approach fits what you want to discuss. Acceptance and commitment therapy, usually called ACT and spoken as the word “act,” is a form of cognitive behavioral therapy that focuses on noticing inner experiences, relating to them differently, and choosing actions connected to personal values. Rather than treating difficult thoughts and feelings as problems that must be argued away, ACT looks at how people can make room for them while paying attention to what matters in the situation at hand.

## Psychological flexibility in ACT

Psychological flexibility is ACT’s central idea: the ability to notice what is happening internally and choose behavior based on the present situation and what matters. It does not mean being endlessly adaptable, agreeable, or unaffected by stress. It means developing a little more room between an internal event—such as worry, self-criticism, a memory, or physical tension—and the next action.

ACT pays attention to patterns that can narrow a person’s options. For example, a person may postpone an important conversation because anxiety shows up, spend long periods debating whether a thought is true, or follow a rigid personal rule even when it no longer fits the circumstances. The work examines what those responses are doing in the short term and how they relate to the direction a person wants to take.

This approach does not require a person to label thoughts as irrational or replace every unpleasant feeling with a pleasant one. Instead, therapy may involve identifying the moment a thought, feeling, urge, or rule begins to organize behavior. From there, the focus is on practicing a response that is more deliberate and workable for that context.

## Defusion: noticing thoughts as thoughts

Cognitive defusion is the ACT skill of recognizing a thought as a thought rather than automatically treating it as a command, prediction, or fact. When people are closely fused with a thought, its wording can seem to define what is possible: “I cannot handle this,” “I have to be certain,” or “If I feel anxious, I should not do it.” Defusion does not ask someone to prove the thought wrong. It asks them to notice how they are relating to it.

A therapist may invite a person to put a phrase in front of a thought, such as “I am noticing the thought that…” This small shift can make the thought’s presence more visible without requiring an argument about its accuracy. Other exercises may involve repeating a phrase until its sound changes, writing it down, naming the mind’s familiar story, or imagining thoughts passing through awareness.

These practices are not meant to make thoughts disappear. Their purpose is to create an opportunity to choose a next step with the thought present. Whether a thought is partly accurate, highly persuasive, or recurring, ACT can explore whether following it automatically moves a person toward or away from the way they want to participate in their life.

## Willingness and present-moment awareness

ACT uses willingness to describe making room for uncomfortable internal experiences when doing so serves a meaningful action. Willingness is different from liking an emotion, approving of a circumstance, or deciding that pain does not matter. It is a choice to stop making the absence of discomfort the condition for every action.

Present-moment awareness supports that choice by bringing attention to what is occurring now. A session might include noticing the contact of feet with the floor, the pace of breathing, sounds in the room, or the particular sensations that accompany tension. These are not tests of concentration, and they do not require a blank mind. They are ways of observing experience with more detail.

ACT may also use gradual, planned contact with situations, emotions, or memories that a person has been avoiding, when that contact fits the therapeutic focus. The point is not to force disclosure or push through without care. Therapist and client discuss the purpose, pace, and boundaries of an exercise, including what would make it useful and what would make it too much for that moment.

## How ACT clarifies what matters

ACT uses values to identify qualities of action a person wants to express, even when circumstances are complicated. Values are not a checklist of accomplishments or goals that can be completed once and for all. They are directions, such as being honest in communication, attentive in a relationship, responsible with commitments, curious in learning, or respectful toward oneself.

Goals can be useful in ACT, but they serve a different role. A goal might be applying for a position, returning a phone call, attending an event, or setting a boundary. Values help answer why that action matters and how a person wants to carry it out. This distinction can be especially useful when a goal is delayed, blocked, or only partly within someone’s control.

Values work is not about selecting a socially approved answer. It can involve looking at roles, relationships, work, health routines, community, creativity, learning, or other areas, then noticing where expectations and personal priorities differ. A therapist may ask concrete questions about what a person wants their behavior to stand for in a particular situation rather than asking for a broad statement about life purpose.

## Putting ACT skills into a practical plan

ACT usually turns broad ideas into small, observable actions that can be considered between appointments. A plan may begin with one current situation: a conversation being put off, a decision that prompts rumination, a routine disrupted by stress, or a pattern of withdrawing when self-criticism appears. The therapist and client can map the internal experiences that show up, the usual response, and an alternative action linked to a chosen value.

ACT can fit that structured style by giving attention to a specific pattern, practicing a skill in session, and identifying a realistic experiment to observe afterward. The plan can be revised based on what happened rather than treated as a rule to follow perfectly.

Between-session practice may be brief and specific. It might involve noticing a recurring thought, using a defusion phrase before a planned action, recording moments of avoidance without criticism, or taking one values-linked step. The purpose is to gather information about the pattern and practice the skill in ordinary settings, not to earn a grade or demonstrate willpower.

- Name the thought, emotion, urge, or body sensation that is present
- Choose one small behavior connected to a stated value
- Review what happened with curiosity at the next appointment

## Frequently asked questions

### Can ACT include work on anxiety, low mood, or stress?

ACT can be used to explore many concerns in which thoughts, feelings, urges, or avoidance shape daily choices. The focus is on the person’s specific pattern and context rather than on applying one exercise to every concern. A therapist can discuss whether ACT concepts fit the issues you want to bring in.

### Why does ACT use metaphors and brief exercises?

Metaphors and experiential exercises can make an abstract process easier to notice in real time. For example, an exercise may highlight the difference between having a thought and obeying it. The purpose is to observe a pattern directly, not to find the one correct interpretation.

### Do I need to meditate outside of sessions for ACT?

No, formal meditation is not required for ACT. Some ACT practices use brief attention exercises, while others focus on everyday moments such as walking into a meeting or pausing before responding to a message. The format can be discussed as part of the treatment plan.

### What is the difference between a value and a goal in ACT?

A value is an ongoing direction for behavior, while a goal is a specific outcome or milestone. For instance, being thoughtful in relationships can be a value, and initiating a particular conversation can be a goal. ACT may use both to make an action plan more concrete.

### Can ACT address self-critical thinking?

ACT can examine how self-critical thoughts show up and what happens when they direct behavior. Defusion, present-moment awareness, and values-based choices are possible ways to approach that pattern. The work does not require pretending that self-critical thoughts are absent or replacing them with forced positive statements.
