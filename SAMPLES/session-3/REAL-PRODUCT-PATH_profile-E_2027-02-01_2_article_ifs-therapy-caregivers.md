# IFS therapy for caregivers

*By Jordan Ellis, Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist, LMFT 121877*

IFS therapy for caregivers is a way of talking about the different reactions, roles, and pressures that can arise when much of daily life is organized around another person’s needs. Caregiving can involve practical tasks, advocacy, vigilance, family dynamics, and decisions that do not wait for an ideal moment. It can also bring up older experiences, strong emotions, or inner conflict that may seem unrelated to the task at hand. Internal Family Systems, usually called IFS, offers a structured way to notice those reactions without treating any one of them as the entire story. At Ellis Counseling, Jordan Ellis, Licensed Marriage and Family Therapist, uses IFS with adults who spend much of their time caring for others and rarely have room to put old experiences down.

## Why might caregiving bring up conflicting reactions?

Caregiving can bring up conflicting reactions because different needs and responsibilities may be active at the same time. Someone may be focused on being dependable while another reaction wants rest, privacy, distance, or practical support. There may also be worry about making a mistake, anger about an unequal division of labor, sadness about a changing relationship, or guilt for wanting anything different.

These reactions do not need to be sorted into “good” and “bad” before they can be discussed. In IFS, the point is to make room to notice how each reaction is trying to manage something important. A highly organized, watchful response may be trying to prevent a problem. A frustrated response may be drawing attention to a limit that has been overlooked. A numb or detached response may appear when the situation feels like too much to take in all at once.

Caregiving is also rarely limited to one role. A person may be a parent, adult child, spouse, partner, sibling, employee, or household manager while navigating another person’s medical, emotional, or practical needs. IFS conversations can include the tension between these roles and the personal history that may be stirred by them. The discussion does not require a person to choose one reaction as the correct one.

## The inner roles that can show up around caregiving

IFS understands recurring inner reactions as parts, meaning recognizable aspects of a person that have particular concerns, emotions, or jobs. This is a practical way to describe experiences such as an inner planner that tracks every detail, a critic that questions every decision, or a protector that keeps personal needs out of view. Parts are not separate personalities, and using this language is optional.

A part may be easy to recognize through its familiar message: “Keep going,” “Do not burden anyone,” “Stay prepared,” or “Do not let your guard down.” It may also show up through a body response, an urge to withdraw, difficulty concentrating, impatience, or a strong need to take charge. In IFS, these details can be treated as information about what is happening internally rather than proof that a person is failing at caregiving.

The work involves becoming curious about a part’s role, what it fears might happen, and what experiences may be connected to its intensity. That inquiry can be especially relevant when a current caregiving situation resembles earlier family expectations, periods of instability, or times when a person had to stay alert to other people’s needs. The conversation can stay with what is present now while also noting those connections when they arise.

## Making room for current responsibilities in IFS

IFS can include the immediate realities of caregiving rather than requiring a person to set them aside for therapy. An upcoming appointment, a conflict with a family member, fatigue after a difficult night, uncertainty about a decision, or the practical work of coordinating care can all be appropriate material. Current demands often shape what has the most attention in a given conversation.

Pacing in IFS means paying attention to how much material is being approached and whether a person wants to continue, slow down, shift focus, or pause. It may involve spending time with a present-day concern before discussing older material. It can also mean noticing when a protective reaction does not want to go further and treating that hesitation as part of the conversation rather than something to push past.

Caregiving schedules and circumstances can change quickly. A session does not have to follow a rigid sequence or focus on the same issue each time. Jordan Ellis works with the material that is most present while keeping the broader caregiving context in view. The therapist may ask what feels most urgent, what has been difficult to put down, and what reactions are asking for attention.

## What happens when guilt, anger, or numbness show up?

Guilt, anger, and numbness can all be explored in IFS as reactions with a context and a purpose. Guilt may appear around rest, boundaries, resentment, or decisions that affect another person. Anger may arise in response to unfair expectations, lack of support, loss, or repeated interruptions. Numbness may show up when emotions or demands feel difficult to hold all at once.

Rather than debating whether an emotion is justified, IFS invites careful attention to how it appears and what it is trying to communicate or prevent. A therapist might ask where the reaction is noticed, what it wants others to understand, or what it is concerned would happen if it did not do its job. There is no requirement to agree with a part’s conclusions in order to listen to its concerns.

This approach can also make room for the complexity of caring about someone and having difficult reactions to the role. Affection, commitment, exhaustion, fear, irritation, grief, and obligation can coexist. Discussing that complexity does not require turning the caregiving relationship into a simple positive or negative story. It allows the conversation to stay specific about what is happening internally and externally.

## What does collaboration look like in IFS?

Collaboration in IFS means the therapist and client decide together what to discuss, how closely to approach it, and when to change direction. The therapist may offer questions or observations, but the person in therapy can say when language does not fit, when a topic feels too far away, or when a practical concern needs attention first. IFS is not a test of whether someone can identify the “right” part or describe an experience in a particular way.

The therapist may use terms such as parts, protectors, or Self because they are common in IFS. Self is a term within the model for a grounded, curious stance toward inner experience. It is not a demand to be calm, detached, or perfectly composed. If another way of describing the experience is more useful, ordinary language can guide the conversation.

It can be useful to ask how a therapist explains IFS, how they handle moments when a person feels overwhelmed or uncertain, and how caregiving responsibilities can be included in the work. These questions clarify the process without requiring a person to arrive with a complete account of their past or a fixed therapy agenda.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is resentment toward caregiving an appropriate topic in IFS?

Yes, resentment can be a relevant topic in IFS. It may be explored alongside the expectations, losses, limits, or unmet needs connected to it, without requiring the caregiving relationship to be reduced to one emotion.

### Can IFS include guilt about setting limits?

Yes, guilt about limits can be discussed in IFS. The conversation may explore the part that feels responsible for continuing to give, as well as any concerns that arise around saying no, asking for support, or changing a routine.

### Can a session focus on one difficult interaction?

Yes, a single interaction can provide a clear starting point. IFS may look at what happened, the reactions that appeared during or afterward, and whether the situation connected with other familiar inner roles.

### Do I have to put caregiving aside to do this work?

No, IFS can include caregiving as an ongoing part of the conversation. Present responsibilities, changing circumstances, and the impact of another person’s needs can all shape what is discussed.

### Can IFS be used when caregiving responsibilities change often?

Yes, the focus of IFS can shift as circumstances change. A conversation may address an immediate concern one week and a longer-standing reaction or relationship pattern at another time.
